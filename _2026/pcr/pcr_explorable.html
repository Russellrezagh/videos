    The Polymerase Chain Reaction

    PCR copies one chosen stretch of DNA, and then copies the copies. Thirty rounds of heating and cooling turn a few hundred molecules into hundreds of billions: enough to see, measure, sequence or clone. The chemistry fits on an index card. The power comes from an exponent.

    In one sentence

    A heat-stable polymerase, two short primers that bracket a target, and a repeating temperature program that separates strands, aims the primers and copies, so each cycle roughly doubles the target.

    Mental model

    Heat to separate, cool to aim, warm to copy. Every new copy becomes a template, so the count multiplies: \(\Nn = \NO(1+\Ef)^{\nn}\).

    You will need

    Base pairing (A–T, G–C), the idea that a DNA strand has a direction, and exponents and logarithms. Everything else is introduced where it is used.

    Note

    This chapter keeps one visual language. Template DNA is blue, new copies are orange, primers are pink, the polymerase is green, the knobs you can turn (temperature, cycle number, efficiency) are purple, the signals we measure (Ct, fluorescence) are yellow, and trouble is red.

    1A single molecule is invisible

    One haploid human genome weighs about 3.3 pg, so 1 ng of DNA holds about 300 copies.

    A human cell carries about 6.4 billion base pairs of DNA: two copies of a 3.1-billion-letter genome. A gene you care about might occupy a few hundred of them. One nanogram of purified human DNA, far too little to see, holds about 300 copies of the genome, and so about 300 copies of a single-copy target, buried in a ten-million-fold excess of everything else.

    No ordinary lab instrument can size, sequence or even detect 300 molecules. A visible band on a gel needs around 1010 of them. PCR closes the gap from the supply side: it leaves the detector alone and makes more of the molecule.

    How many more, and how fast? Doubling is the natural unit. Since \(2^{10} \approx 10^{3}\), every ten doublings buy a factor of a thousand, and every factor of ten costs \(\log_2 10 \approx 3.3\) doublings. Figure 1.1 puts both scales on one axis.

    2The molecular cast

    PCR is DNA replication with the cell removed. To see why each ingredient is there, we need one structural fact about DNA and one rule about the enzyme that copies it.

    BackgroundDNA in sixty seconds

    DNA is a chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide is a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate and one of four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) or thymine (T). Sugars and phosphates alternate to form the backbone, and the bases stick out to one side. Two chains wind into a double helix with their bases paired inside, A with T and G with C. The pairs are held by hydrogen bonds between partners and by stacking between neighboring pairs.

    Because the pairing rule is strict, each strand fully specifies its partner. That is what makes copying possible: separate the strands, and each one is a template for a new partner.

    2.1Two strands, two directions

    Each strand has a direction. Its two ends are named 5′ and 3′ after carbon atoms of the sugar, and the two strands of a helix run opposite ways. Wherever one strand reads 5′→3′ from left to right, its partner reads 5′→3′ from right to left. Biologists call this antiparallel.

    The whole chapter hangs on one rule. A DNA polymerase can only add a nucleotide to the free 3′-OH end of a strand that is already paired with a template. It reads the template 3′→5′, builds the new strand 5′→3′, and cannot start a strand from nothing.

    Three consequences follow, and each returns later. PCR needs primers to give the enzyme a starting 3′ end. The two primers must point toward each other across the target. And a mismatch at a primer’s 3′ end is fatal, while a mismatch at its 5′ end is tolerated.

    Pause and predict

    Suppose the tube contains only the forward primer. Starting from one double-stranded template, how many new strands does the reaction make in 30 cycles?

    Show the reasoning

    The forward primer has the sequence of the top strand, so it pairs only with bottom-sense strands. The original bottom strand is copied once per cycle, and every copy is top-sense, which the forward primer cannot bind. Add the reverse primer, which pairs with top-sense strands, and the loop closes: every product of one primer is a template for the other. That feedback is the chain in polymerase chain reaction, and it turns \(\NO + \nn\) into \(\NO \cdot 2^{\nn}\).

    3One cycle, three temperatures

    A thermocycler is a metal block that can change temperature by tens of degrees in seconds. Each cycle runs three steps, and the only thing that changes between them is the temperature. Temperature decides which physical process wins: melting, binding or copying.

    Notice two things. After one cycle the copies are longer than the target: in cycle 1 nothing tells the polymerase where to stop, so it runs on past the far primer site, producing long products. In cycle 2 those long copies become templates, and copying one of them stops exactly at its end, which is the other primer. The first strands of exactly the target length appear there. Section 4 counts what happens next.

    3.1Why these temperatures

    • Denature, 94–98 °C, 15–30 s. Above the melting temperature of the template (about 85–95 °C for genomic DNA in PCR buffer, higher when GC-rich) the strands separate. Taq survives because it evolved in hot springs: its half-life at 95 °C is roughly 40 minutes, enough for 30–40 cycles.
    • Anneal, about 50–65 °C, 15–30 s. Cool enough for the primers to pair, warm enough that they pair only where the match is good. The usual starting point is a few degrees below the primers’ melting temperature.
    • Extend, 72 °C, about 1 minute per kilobase. Close to Taq’s optimum (75–80 °C) while cool enough for the primers to stay bound. Taq adds tens of nucleotides per second, so the rule of thumb is generous.

    Pause and think

    When the tube cools, each long template strand could re-pair with its own partner, or bind a primer. Re-pairing would end the cycle with nothing copied. Why do the primers win?

    Show the reasoning

    Concentration. Ten nanograms of human DNA in 50 µL is about 3,000 copies of a target, roughly 10−16 M. Primers at 0.5 µM outnumber them about five billion to one, and the rate at which a strand meets a partner is proportional to the partner’s concentration. Short primers also diffuse quickly and need only a 20-base match to form a stable duplex.

    Late in the reaction, though, products reach about 3 nM. Now product strands find their own partners almost as fast as they find primers, and that competition is one reason amplification slows (§5.4).

    3.2Melting temperature and primer design

    The popular story credits G–C’s third hydrogen bond. Measurements suggest base stacking contributes more to the difference.

    The melting temperature \(\Tm\) of a duplex is the temperature at which half of it has come apart. Longer duplexes melt higher, and GC-rich ones melt higher than AT-rich ones. Two rules of thumb estimate \(\Tm\) for a short primer of \(N\) bases:

    \[ \Tm \approx 2\,(\#A + \#T) + 4\,(\#G + \#C)\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \qquad (N \lt 14) \] \[ \Tm \approx 64.9 + 41\,\frac{\#G + \#C - 16.4}{N}\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \qquad (N \ge 14) \]

    Both are approximations. Primer-design software uses nearest-neighbor thermodynamics, which accounts for which bases sit next to each other, for salt and Mg²⁺, and for primer concentration; its numbers can differ from these by several degrees. For seeing how the knobs interact, the simple formula is enough.

    Specificity is a tug-of-war set by the annealing temperature \(\Ta\) relative to \(\Tm\). Set \(\Ta\) too low and primers stick to near-matches elsewhere in the genome, or to each other. Set it too high and they barely stick at all. The bench below lets you feel this, together with the 3′ rule from Figure 2.1.

    4Counting copies: where the exponential comes from

    Here is the claim that makes PCR powerful: every strand made in one cycle is a template in the next. If every template is copied, the number of target molecules doubles each cycle, so after \(\nn\) cycles one molecule has become \(2^{\nn}\).

    Does “every copy is a template” make the doubling feel inevitable?

    A picture

    An analogy, and where it breaks

    Compound interest at 100% per period: interest earns interest, so the balance doubles every period. The analogy captures the feedback, output becoming input. It breaks in two places. Money has no reagents to run out of, while PCR stalls when primers and dNTPs run low (§5.4). And a bank never forgets a dollar, while the polymerase misses some templates every cycle, so the real multiplier is \(1+\Ef\), a little below 2 (§5.2).

    4.1Long products and short products

    Doubling counts all strands, but not every strand has the length we want. The original template runs far past the target in both directions. Figure 3.1 showed that cycle-1 copies start at a primer and run on past the other site: these long products have one defined end. Copies of exactly the target length, bounded by both primers, come later.

    Pause and predict

    In which cycle does the first double-stranded molecule of exactly the target length appear, with both strands bounded by the two primers?

    Show the reasoning

    Track three strand types. Copying an original strand (O) gives a long strand (L). Copying a long strand gives a short, exact strand (S), because copying stops at the template’s end, which is a primer site. Copying an S gives another S. Cycle 1: O→L. Cycle 2: O→L and L→S, so the first S strands appear, each paired with the L it was copied from. Cycle 3: S→S, and an S paired with an S is the first exact duplex.

    The table’s last column has a closed form. Count by exclusion:

    \[ \#\,\text{exact} = \#\,\text{all} - \#\,\text{with an original} - \#\,\text{with a long strand} \]

    Every duplex at cycle n is one template plus the copy made from it during cycle n.

    \[ = 2^{\nn} - 2 - 2(\nn - 1) \]

    Two originals, each copied once per cycle. Long strands appear two per cycle, so 2(n − 1) were around to be templates in cycle n.

    \[ = 2^{\nn} - 2\nn \]

    Exponential minus linear. Check: n = 3 gives 8 − 6 = 2.

    \[ \nn = 30:\quad 2^{30} - 60 = 1\,073\,741\,764 \]

    Long products become a rounding error: 60 duplexes among a billion.

    So the product is defined by the primers, not by the template. Whatever the template looked like beyond the primer sites, after 30 cycles essentially every molecule in the tube starts at one primer and ends at the other. This is also why the 5′ end of a primer can carry extra sequence: from cycle 2 on, the primer itself is part of the template.

    5The mathematics of amplification

    5.1Ideal doubling

    Write \(\NO\) for the number of target copies before cycling and \(\Nn\) for the number after \(n\) cycles. If every copy is copied in every cycle,

    \[ \Nn = \NO \cdot 2^{\nn}. \]

    The count is a geometric sequence: each cycle multiplies it by the same factor.

    5.2Efficiency

    Real cycles miss some templates: a primer fails to bind in time, a polymerase stalls, a strand re-pairs before it is primed. Let the efficiency \(\Ef\) be the fraction of templates copied per cycle, between 0 and 1. A cycle turns \(N\) molecules into \(N + \Ef N\), so

    \[ \Nn = \NO\,(1+\Ef)^{\nn}. \]

    Well-designed assays run at \(\Ef \approx 0.9\)–\(1.0\). The difference looks small per cycle and is not small after thirty.

    Worked example 5.1

    Start with \(\NO = 100\) copies and run 30 cycles.

    1. At \(\Ef = 1\): \(N_{30} = 100 \cdot 2^{30} = 1.07 \times 10^{11}\).
    2. At \(\Ef = 0.95\): \(N_{30} = 100 \cdot 1.95^{30} = 5.02 \times 10^{10}\).
    3. The ratio is \((2/1.95)^{30} = 1.0256^{30} = 2.14\). A 2.5% shortfall per cycle compounds into a factor of two, because the exponent multiplies every per-cycle loss together.

    5.3Logarithms make it a line

    Take the logarithm of both sides and the exponent comes down:

    \[ \log_{10}\Nn = \log_{10}\NO + \nn \cdot \log_{10}(1+\Ef). \]

    Plotted against cycle number on a log axis, the growth phase is a straight line. Its slope is \(\log_{10}(1+\Ef)\) decades per cycle (0.301 at \(\Ef = 1\)) and its intercept is \(\log_{10}\NO\). The intercept is what we want to measure; the slope tells us how well the reaction is running. This is the entire basis of quantitative PCR.

    5.4The plateau

    Exponential growth cannot last. After 25–35 cycles the reaction slows and stops at a plateau of roughly 1011–1012 copies, for several reasons acting together:

    • Primers and dNTPs are consumed; by the end, much of them is built into product.
    • The polymerase is outnumbered: there are more templates than enzyme molecules can copy within one extension step.
    • Products re-anneal. At nanomolar concentrations each product strand finds its full-length partner almost as fast as it finds a primer: the race from §3, now lost.
    • By-products such as pyrophosphate accumulate, and the enzyme slowly loses activity.

    A convenient description is the logistic map, in which the effective efficiency falls as the count approaches a ceiling \(\KK\):

    \[ N_{\nn+1} = \Nn + \Ef\,\Nn\left(1 - \frac{\Nn}{\KK}\right). \]

    This describes the curve’s shape; it is not a mechanism. It is what Figure 5.1 simulates.

    5.5The threshold cycle

    The MIQE reporting guidelines call it the quantification cycle, Cq. Instrument software also says Ct or Cp.

    Real-time PCR (qPCR) measures fluorescence every cycle and reports the threshold cycle \(\Ct\): the fractional cycle at which the signal crosses a threshold \(\Nt\) set inside the exponential phase. Because \(\Ct\) is read where every curve is still a straight line on a log axis, it carries the information about \(\NO\) that the plateau erases.

    \[ \NO\,(1+\Ef)^{\Ct} = \Nt \]

    Definition: after Ct cycles, the copies reach the threshold.

    \[ \log_{10}\NO + \Ct\,\log_{10}(1+\Ef) = \log_{10}\Nt \]

    Take log₁₀ of both sides. The product becomes a sum and the exponent comes down.

    \[ \Ct = \frac{\log_{10}\Nt - \log_{10}\NO}{\log_{10}(1+\Ef)} \]

    Solve for Ct.

    \[ \Ct = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{\log_{10}(1+\Ef)}}_{\text{slope}}\,\log_{10}\NO + \underbrace{\frac{\log_{10}\Nt}{\log_{10}(1+\Ef)}}_{\text{intercept}} \]

    Separate the terms: Ct is a straight line in log₁₀ N₀.

    \[ \Ef = 10^{-1/\text{slope}} - 1 \]

    Run a dilution series, fit the line, and read the efficiency off its slope.

    At \(\Ef = 1\) the slope is \(-1/\log_{10}2 = -3.32\): each ten-fold dilution delays the crossing by 3.32 cycles, and each two-fold dilution by exactly one.

    Worked example 5.2

    A ten-fold dilution series of a standard gives a line with slope −3.45.

    1. \(\Ef = 10^{1/3.45} - 1 = 0.949\): about 95% efficiency, inside the commonly accepted 90–110% window.
    2. A slope of −3.1 would give \(\Ef = 10^{1/3.1} - 1 = 1.10\). Efficiency above 100% is impossible for the chemistry, so it flags an artifact, often inhibitors in the concentrated samples that delay their Ct and flatten the line (§8).

    Pause and predict

    Two patient samples run with the same assay. Sample X crosses the threshold at \(\Ct = 18\), sample Y at \(\Ct = 32\). Which contained more target, and roughly by how much?

    5.6Relative quantification

    Often you need only how much a gene’s level changed between conditions, not absolute copy numbers. The ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) normalizes the target gene to a reference gene in each sample, then compares samples:

    \[ \Delta\Ct = \Ct^{\,\text{target}} - \Ct^{\,\text{ref}}, \qquad \Delta\Delta\Ct = \Delta\Ct^{\,\text{treated}} - \Delta\Ct^{\,\text{control}}, \qquad \text{fold change} = (1+\Ef)^{-\Delta\Delta \Ct} \approx 2^{-\Delta\Delta \Ct}. \]

    Worked example 5.3

    Control: target Ct 25.0, reference Ct 18.0, so ΔCt = 7.0. Treated: target Ct 22.5, reference Ct 18.2, so ΔCt = 4.3.

    Then ΔΔCt = 4.3 − 7.0 = −2.7 and the fold change is \(2^{2.7} = 6.5\): the gene is about 6.5 times higher in the treated sample. The shortcut \(2^{-\Delta\Delta C_t}\) assumes both assays run near 100% efficiency. When they differ, use the efficiency-corrected form (Pfaffl, 2001).

    Deeper

    5.7Chance in the first cycles

    The equation \(\Nn = \NO(1+\Ef)^{\nn}\) describes an average. Each molecule is copied or not, by chance, with probability \(\Ef\). When \(\NO\) is large the luck averages out. When it is small, one miss in cycle 1 delays everything by a full cycle. Mathematically, PCR is a Galton–Watson branching process: every molecule independently leaves behind one or two molecules.

    _2026/pcr/pcr_branching.py

    In [1]:

    import math
import random


def binomial(n: int, p: float, rng: random.Random) -> int:
    """Number of molecules (out of n) that get copied this cycle."""
    if n < 1000:
        return sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(n))
    # Normal approximation is accurate once n is large.
    return max(0, round(rng.gauss(n * p, math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p)))))


def amplify(n0: int, E: float, cycles: int, rng: random.Random) -> int:
    """Galton-Watson PCR: each molecule is copied with probability E per cycle."""
    N = n0
    for _ in range(cycles):
        N += binomial(N, E, rng)
    return N


def ct(n0: int, E: float, rng: random.Random, early: int = 10, Nt: float = 1e10) -> float:
    """Simulate the random early cycles exactly, then grow deterministically to Nt."""
    N = amplify(n0, E, early, rng)
    return early + math.log(Nt / N) / math.log(1 + E)

E = 0.90

print("Eight reactions, each starting from ONE molecule:")
for run in range(8):

    print(f"  run {run}:  N_10 = {N10:4d}   Ct = {10 + math.log(1e10 / N10) / math.log(1 + E):5.2f}")
print(f"  expected N_10 = (1+E)^10 = {(1 + E) ** 10:.0f}\n")

print("Spread of Ct over 500 replicates:")
for n0 in (1, 10, 100, 10_000):

    print(f"  N0 = {n0:6d}:  mean Ct {m:5.2f}   sd {sd:.2f} cycles")

    Two lessons. Starting from a single molecule, \(\Ct\) values scatter with a standard deviation of about half a cycle, and the scatter shrinks roughly as \(1/\sqrt{\NO}\): 0.46, 0.12 and 0.04 cycles for 1, 10 and 100 copies. And this is only the copying noise. Before cycling starts, pipetting from a sample that averages 3 copies per reaction puts zero copies in the tube \(e^{-3} \approx 5\%\) of the time (Poisson sampling), which is why replicates near the detection limit disagree.

    Deeper

    5.8Copying errors

    Taq has no 3′→5′ proofreading exonuclease, so a wrong base, once added, stays. Error rates are reported per base per doubling. Published values for Taq span roughly 10−5 to 10−4 depending on the assay, and proofreading enzymes such as Pfu or engineered high-fidelity polymerases are one to two orders of magnitude more accurate.

    With error rate \(\eps\), amplicon length \(\LL\) and \(\dd = \nn\log_2(1+\Ef)\) doublings, the expected number of errors per product molecule is about \(\eps\LL\dd\). If errors are rare and independent, the fraction of products with no error at all is

    \[ P(\text{error-free}) \approx e^{-\eps\,\LL\,\dd}. \]

    Errors grow linearly with the number of doublings while copies grow exponentially, which is why PCR products are usable at all. For cloning a long gene, though, the linear term matters, and high-fidelity enzymes earn their price.

    6Reading the result

    Amplification is half the experiment. The other half is finding out what was made, and how much.

    6.1Gel electrophoresis

    A visible band needs roughly 10 ng of DNA: about 2×10¹⁰ copies of a 500-bp product.

    DNA’s phosphate backbone carries one negative charge per nucleotide, so in an electric field every fragment is pulled toward the positive electrode. Charge and friction both grow with length, so in free solution all fragments would move at about the same speed. An agarose gel breaks the tie: it is a mesh through which short fragments slip and long ones snake slowly. Over a useful range, the distance a fragment travels falls roughly linearly with the logarithm of its length. That is why a size ladder (fragments of known length) is spaced evenly in log(bp), as in Figure 3.2. A dye such as ethidium bromide or a SYBR stain fluoresces when bound to DNA and makes each band glow.

    6.2Watching it happen: real-time PCR

    Quantitative PCR adds a fluorescent reporter and a camera, and reads the tube every cycle. Two chemistries dominate.

    Intercalating dyes such as SYBR Green fluoresce strongly only when bound to double-stranded DNA, so the signal tracks total double-stranded product. They are cheap and work with any primer pair, but they cannot tell the target from a primer-dimer. The fix uses the physics of §3: after cycling, the instrument heats the tube slowly and watches the signal drop as products melt. A specific product melts at its own \(\Tm\) in one sharp step; primer-dimers, being short, melt lower.

    Hydrolysis probes (TaqMan) add a third oligo, the probe, that binds inside the amplicon and carries a fluorescent reporter at one end and a quencher at the other. While the probe is intact, the quencher absorbs the reporter’s energy. Taq has a second activity, a 5′→3′ exonuclease, which chops the probe apart when the polymerase runs into it.

    6.3RNA first: RT-PCR

    Without a no-reverse-transcriptase control, contaminating genomic DNA can masquerade as RNA signal.

    Taq copies DNA, not RNA. To detect an RNA virus or to measure gene expression from messenger RNA, a reverse transcriptase, an enzyme borrowed from retroviruses, first copies the RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). PCR then amplifies the cDNA. Combined with real-time detection this is RT-qPCR, the reference test for SARS-CoV-2 since early 2020. The Ct values reported for such tests follow the arithmetic of §5: every 3.3 cycles is about a ten-fold difference in viral RNA, and Ct values in the high 30s are near the single-copy limit.

    6.4Counting molecules one at a time: digital PCR

    Ct measures template indirectly, through a calibration curve. Digital PCR counts molecules. Split the sample into thousands of tiny partitions (droplets or wells) so that most hold zero or one template molecule, run PCR to the end, and count the partitions that light up. Each partition gives a yes-or-no answer, hence “digital”.

    The catch is that a partition with two or three molecules lights up exactly like one with a single molecule. If molecules land independently with an average of \(\lam\) per partition, the count in each partition follows a Poisson distribution, and the chance that a partition receives none is \(e^{-\lam}\). So the expected fraction of positive partitions is

    \[ \pp = 1 - e^{-\lam} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lam = -\ln(1-\pp). \]

    Measure \(\pp\), invert, and multiply by the number of partitions to get the number of molecules, with no standard curve and no assumption about efficiency.

    8Failure modes and edge cases

    PCR’s sensitivity is also its main weakness. A method that can find one molecule will just as happily find the wrong one.

    Warning: contamination

    A finished reaction holds about 1011 copies in 50 µL, which is 2×106 copies per nanoliter. A single invisible aerosol droplet from an opened tube can seed a new reaction with more template than a genuine low-copy sample. Labs keep pre- and post-PCR work physically separate, run a no-template control (NTC) in every experiment, and often build products with dUTP instead of dTTP so that the enzyme uracil-DNA glycosylase can destroy carried-over amplicons before the next run.

    Primer-dimers and mispriming. Primers that pair with each other, or with near-matches elsewhere, make short or wrong products (Figure 3.2). Remedies: better primer design, a higher \(\Ta\), touchdown cycling, hot-start enzymes.

    Inhibitors. Heme from blood, heparin, humic acids from soil, EDTA (which captures Mg²⁺) and simply too much template DNA all slow the polymerase. A telltale sign is a standard curve that is too shallow: the concentrated samples are inhibited, their Ct comes late, the slope is flatter than −3.32, and the computed efficiency exceeds 100%.

    Difficult templates. GC-rich regions resist melting and fold into secondary structures; additives such as DMSO or betaine help. Products longer than about 5 kb need long-range enzyme blends and longer extension times.

    Skewed mixtures. When several templates compete (microbial communities, two alleles), small differences in efficiency compound. Two templates that start 1:1 at \(\Ef = 0.95\) and \(\Ef = 0.90\) end 2.2:1 after 30 cycles, because \((1.95/1.90)^{30} = 2.18\). Partially extended strands can also prime on a related template and create chimeras.

    Too few molecules. Near the detection limit, Poisson sampling decides whether the target is in the tube at all (§5.7), so a negative result at low copy number is weak evidence of absence.

    Genomic DNA in RT-PCR. Run a no-RT control, or design primers that span an exon–exon junction so that only spliced cDNA amplifies.

    9A short history

    1. 1953Watson and Crick, building on X-ray data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins, propose the double helix. Complementary pairing implies a copying mechanism.
    2. 1956Arthur Kornberg isolates DNA polymerase from E. coli.
    3. 1969Thomas Brock and Hudson Freeze isolate Thermus aquaticus from a Yellowstone hot spring.
    4. 1971Kleppe, Khorana and colleagues describe repair replication of short DNA with two primers, a conceptual precursor.
    5. 1976Chien, Edgar and Trela purify a heat-stable DNA polymerase from T. aquaticus.
    6. 1983Kary Mullis, at Cetus Corporation, conceives of cycling two primers to amplify a target exponentially.
    7. 1985Saiki and colleagues publish the first application, a test for sickle-cell anemia. Fresh polymerase (Klenow fragment) had to be added after every heat step.
    8. 1988Saiki and colleagues switch to Taq polymerase, and PCR becomes automatable.
    9. 1991Holland and colleagues use Taq’s 5′→3′ exonuclease to release a probe signal, the basis of TaqMan.
    10. 1992Higuchi and colleagues watch amplification in real time with a fluorescent dye and a video camera.
    11. 1993Mullis shares the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Michael Smith (site-directed mutagenesis).
    12. 1999Vogelstein and Kinzler name and demonstrate digital PCR.
    13. 2020Corman and colleagues publish an RT-qPCR assay for SARS-CoV-2 within weeks of the first genome sequence, and Ct values enter the news.

    10Where PCR shows up

    • Diagnostics. Pathogen detection by (RT-)qPCR; genetic tests for inherited variants.
    • Forensics. Kits co-amplify about 20 short tandem repeat (STR) loci, and the repeat counts at each locus make up a DNA profile. Because the amplicons are short, degraded samples still work.
    • Cloning and engineering. 5′ tails on primers add restriction sites or tags, and deliberately mismatched primers introduce mutations. Both exploit the 3′ rule of Figure 2.1.
    • Sequencing. Sanger sequencing reads PCR products, and most next-generation library preparations include a PCR step to attach adapters.
    • Ancient and environmental DNA. Fragments from bones, sediments or seawater can be amplified, which makes contamination control (§8) decisive.

    10.1Connections

    Cellular DNA replication. PCR keeps the copying chemistry and replaces the rest of the machinery with physics and synthesis:

    JobIn a cellIn PCR
    Separate the strandsHelicase unwinds the helixHeat, 95 °C
    Start new strandsPrimase lays down short RNA primersSynthetic DNA primers
    CopyReplicative polymerases with proofreadingTaq (no proofreading) or a high-fidelity enzyme
    What gets copiedThe whole genome, once per divisionOne primer-defined region, 25–40 times
    Join fragmentsLigase seals Okazaki fragmentsNot needed: both strands are copied continuously from fixed starts

    Exponential growth elsewhere. Compound interest, bacterial growth and the early phase of an epidemic all follow \(N_0(1+r)^n\) with \(r\) the growth per step, and for the same reason a logarithmic axis turns them into straight lines.

    Poisson counting. Digital PCR, limiting-dilution assays and photon counting share the same inversion, \(\lambda = -\ln(1-p)\).

    Branching processes. The randomness of the first cycles obeys the mathematics Galton and Watson developed for the extinction of surnames, which also describes neutron chain reactions.

    Isothermal amplification. LAMP and RPA keep the exponential and drop the thermocycler. The transfer question below asks what that costs.

    11Synthesis

    PCR is a feedback loop built from one chemical rule. A polymerase extends only a paired 3′ end, so two primers pointing at each other define a target. Every copy made from one primer is a template for the other, and temperature switches the reaction between separating, aiming and copying. The loop makes the target grow geometrically while everything else grows linearly or not at all.

    Key relations

    \[ \Nn = \NO(1+\Ef)^{\nn} \qquad \Ct = \frac{\log_{10}\Nt - \log_{10}\NO}{\log_{10}(1+\Ef)} \qquad \lam = -\ln(1-\pp) \]

    The picture to keep: on a log axis the amplification curve is a straight ramp whose position, not its height, records how much you started with. Ten times more template slides the ramp about 3.3 cycles to the left. The plateau at the top is the same for everyone.

    Transfer question

    LAMP amplifies DNA at a constant 60–65 °C using a polymerase that peels apart a double strand as it copies (strand displacement). Which of PCR’s three temperature steps has it made unnecessary? Would you still expect a threshold-crossing readout like Ct?

    Show an answer

    It removes denaturation: strand displacement does the job heat did, so binding and copying happen at one temperature. The growth is still a feedback loop in which products seed further copying, so the signal rises exponentially and then plateaus. Real-time LAMP instruments report a time to threshold that plays the role of Ct, measured in minutes instead of cycles.

    11.1Exercises

    1. How many cycles take 50 copies to 1010 at \(\Ef = 0.92\)?
      Answer

      \(\nn = \log(10^{10}/50)/\log 1.92 = 8.30/0.283 \approx 29.3\), so 30 cycles.

    2. A dilution series has slope −3.1. What efficiency does that imply, and what would you check?
      Answer

      \(\Ef = 10^{1/3.1} - 1 \approx 1.10\), impossible for the chemistry. Check the most concentrated standards for inhibition (re-run them diluted), and look for pipetting errors or primer-dimer signal if you use a dye.

    3. A digital PCR run has 20,000 partitions, 4,000 of them positive. How many template molecules were loaded?
      Answer

      \(\pp = 0.2\), so \(\lam = -\ln 0.8 = 0.223\) and molecules ≈ 20,000 × 0.223 ≈ 4,460: about 12% more than the 4,000 positives.
