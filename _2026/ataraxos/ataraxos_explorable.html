    Decisions in the Dark

    In Stratego, you cannot see the ranks of the enemy pieces. For decades, this hidden information stopped the methods that won chess and Go. In 2025, an AI named Ataraxos beat the best Stratego player in history, 15 wins to 1 loss. It used a few thousand dollars of compute. This page explains how, from first principles.

    The paper in one sentence

    Ataraxos learns by self-play with damped updates, models the hidden pieces with a belief network, and does search as one more learning step.

    Mental model

    Guess the hidden information well. Act unpredictably. Learn slowly enough that you do not chase your own tail.

    1Games and information

    Game theory is the mathematics of decisions that affect each other. Before we look at Stratego, we need four words: game, perfect, complete and equilibrium. This section gives the first three. Section 3 gives the fourth.

    1.1What a game is

    A game has players, rules, decisions and results. Each player selects actions. The rules change the state of the game after each action. At the end, each player gets a result, called a payoff.

    • In a two-player zero-sum game, one player wins what the other player loses. Chess, Go, poker and Stratego are examples.
    • In a fully cooperative game, all players get the same payoff. Hanabi is an example.
    • In a team game, teams compete, and each team member must help the team. Bridge and dou dizhu are examples.

    A policy is a rule that tells a player what to do in each situation. A policy can give one fixed action. It can also give a probability to each action. A policy of the second type is a mixed strategy.

    Von Neumann and Morgenstern built this vocabulary in 1944. Kuhn added information sets in 1953.

    1.2Perfect and imperfect information

    In a game of perfect information, each player sees everything that happened before each decision. Chess and Go are games of perfect information. The board shows all.

    In a game of imperfect information, a player does not see some earlier events. Another player can know these events. The paper calls this hidden information: information that one agent has and another agent does not have.

    Kuhn gave the precise tool in 1953. An information set is a group of game states that a player cannot tell apart. In chess, each information set holds exactly one state. In Stratego, one information set can hold more than 1033 states, because the opponent can arrange the hidden pieces in that many ways.

    Chance does not make information imperfect. In backgammon, dice add chance, but both players see the dice. Backgammon is a game of perfect information with chance.

    1.3Complete and incomplete information

    People often confuse a second pair of terms. A game has complete information if all players know the rules, the possible actions and the payoffs of all players. It has incomplete information if a player does not know the payoffs or the “type” of another player.

    Stratego has complete information, because the rules and the goal are known to all. It has imperfect information, because the set-ups are hidden. So the question “Is Stratego a game of complete information?” has the answer yes. The question “Is it a game of perfect information?” has the answer no.

    Harsanyi connected the two pairs in 1967. He added a chance player, “Nature”, that selects each player’s type at the start. After this change, incomplete information becomes imperfect information about a chance move. For this work, Harsanyi shared the 1994 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics with Nash and Selten.

    1.4How much is hidden

    The amount of hidden information decides which methods work. The paper makes this point clearly. Older methods examine every possible hidden state together. Their cost grows with the number of these states.

    2Stratego, the test case

    Stratego is a board wargame for two players. People call it “military chess with secrets”. It has two phases. First, each player arranges 40 pieces in secret. Then the players move one piece per turn, as in chess.

    Stratego comes from the French game L’Attaque (patent 1908). The Dutch designer Jacques Mogendorff created the modern game before 1942.

    When a piece moves onto an enemy piece, a battle occurs. Both ranks become public. The piece with the higher rank wins. If the ranks are equal, both pieces go. A player wins if they capture the enemy Flag, or if the enemy cannot move.

    Why is Stratego so hard for AI? There are four reasons.

    1. Much hidden information. More than 1033 set-ups per player, as Section 1.4 shows.
    2. Long games. A game often lasts hundreds of moves. DeepMind estimated 10535 possible game states, compared with approximately 10360 for Go.
    3. Information is an asset. A revealed Marshal becomes a target for the Spy. Good players hide their ranks and bluff.
    4. Two different tasks. The set-up is one large secret decision. The move phase is a long series of small decisions.

    In 2022, DeepMind published DeepNash. It reached the level of strong human experts, with a training budget of millions of dollars. It did not beat the best players. Stratego was perhaps the last classical game in which well-funded AI did not reach superhuman level.

    3Why hidden information breaks the usual recipe

    3.1The recipe for perfect information

    In chess and Go, the correct action has a simple definition: select the move that gives the best resulting position. The value of a position is the expected result if both players play well from that position. A program can learn this value and search ahead with it.

    AlphaZero (2017) uses exactly this recipe. A neural network gives a policy and a value. A tree search looks ahead and improves the policy. Self-play makes new training data. The paper calls the combination of reinforcement learning and search “a powerful recipe”. For perfect information, the recipe works because a position has one value.

    3.2The value of a decision depends on other decisions

    With hidden information, a position does not have one value. The paper states the reason. The value of a decision depends on the policies that the players use after the decision. It also depends on the policies that they used before it, and on the decisions that they could have made but did not make. These are counterfactual decisions.

    “The more you bluff, the more your opponent expects it, and the less each bluff is worth. It’s not obvious how to reason about that.”Samuel Sokota, first author, to MIT News

    A small game makes this concrete. We use it again in Sections 4 and 5.

    Is it clear why an action can change the value of a different action?

    An everyday picture

    Think of a penalty kick in football. The kicker selects left or right. The goalkeeper selects left or right at the same time. If the kicker always goes left, the goalkeeper always dives left. So the value of “kick left” depends on how often the kicker kicks right. Economists measured real penalty kicks in 2003. Professional players mix their choices at close to the rates that game theory predicts.

    A story

    In a Sherlock Holmes story, a dog did not bark during the night. Holmes concludes that the dog knew the intruder. An action that did not occur gave the information. Stratego is the same. If an enemy piece does not attack your Scout, you learn something about its rank. But what you learn depends on how often that player usually attacks.

    3.3Mixed strategies, equilibrium and exploitability

    In the bluff game, every pure policy loses value. If you never bluff, the opponent retreats from every advance. If you always bluff, the opponent stands every time. The best policy mixes. It bluffs often enough to keep the opponent unsure, but not so often that standing becomes safe.

    A Nash equilibrium is a set of policies, one for each player, in which no player can gain by a change of their own policy alone. In the bluff game, the equilibrium is b* ≈ 0.381 and s* ≈ 0.286. Your expected payoff there is ≈ 0.107.

    In a two-player zero-sum game, the equilibrium has a strong property. It guarantees its value against every opponent. Von Neumann proved this in 1928 (the minimax theorem). The exploitability of a policy measures how far it is from this guarantee. It is the amount that a perfect opponent can take from you, beyond the equilibrium value. Ataraxos does not try to read or exploit its opponent. It tries to have low exploitability.

    \[ \pBel(\text{General}\mid\text{advance}) = \frac{0.25}{0.25 + 0.75\,\pB} \]

    The opponent’s belief after an advance. More bluffs make an advance less frightening.

    \[ \pQ(\text{stand}) = 0.5 - 1.5\,\pBel, \qquad \pQ(\text{retreat}) = -0.2 \]

    The opponent’s action values. They depend on b only through the belief.

    \[ \pQ(\text{bluff}) = 0.2 - 0.7\,\pS, \qquad \pQ(\text{stay}) = 0 \]

    Your Scout’s action values. They depend on how often the opponent stands.

    \[ \pB^{*} = 0.381, \qquad \pS^{*} = 0.286 \]

    At equilibrium, each player is indifferent: both actions of each player have equal value.

    3.4Beliefs: what the moves tell you

    A belief is a probability for each possible value of the hidden information. When you see a new action, you update the belief with Bayes’ rule:

    \[ \pBel(\pH \mid \pO) \;=\; \frac{\pBel(\pO \mid \pH)\,\pBel(\pH)}{\sum_{\pH'} \pBel(\pO \mid \pH')\,\pBel(\pH')} \]

    Here h is the hidden information (for example, a rank) and o is what you observe (for example, a move). The important term is P(o | h): how often a piece of rank h makes move o. This term comes from the opponent’s policy. So your belief depends on the policy of your opponent. Figure 3.2 lets you feel this.

    3.5Why the older solutions do not scale

    Poker AI solved the problem of counterfactuals in a different way. DeepStack and Libratus (2017), Pluribus (2019) and ReBeL (2020) use public belief states. A public belief state lists, for each possible private hand, the probability that each player holds it. The program then solves a small game over all these hands together.

    This works in Texas hold’em, with 1,326 possible hands. The paper explains the limit: the cost of these transformations grows with the amount of hidden information. A list over 1033 Stratego set-ups is not possible.

    A second old method is perfect-information Monte Carlo (PIMC). It samples guesses of the hidden information. It solves each guess as if all information were public, and then it averages the results. Bridge programs used it in the 1990s. In 1998, Frank and Basin showed its main error, strategy fusion. In each sampled world, the program pretends it will know the hidden information later. A real player does not know it. Section 5 shows this error in a lab.

    A third method uses no search at all. DeepNash learned a policy by regularized self-play and played without search. It reached expert level but not superhuman level.

    Deeper

    3.6Counterfactual regret minimization

    Most poker programs use counterfactual regret minimization (CFR), from Zinkevich and colleagues (2007). At each information set, CFR asks: how much better would I have done if I had always played action a here? It calls this the regret of a. Then it plays each action in proportion to its positive regret.

    CFR has a guarantee: the average of its policies over time approaches a Nash equilibrium. But the current policy can swing around. To use the average, a program must store or learn it, and must weigh past data correctly. The paper calls these steps “trajectory importance reweighting and policy averaging”. Ataraxos avoids them. Its current policy itself converges, because of damping (Section 4).

    4Learning by self-play, with damping

    4.1Self-play reinforcement learning

    In self-play, a program plays games against copies of itself. It starts with no knowledge (“tabula rasa”). After each batch of games, it moves probability toward actions that led to good results. This is reinforcement learning.

    Three quantities control each update:

    • The value v: the expected result from a position, under the current policies.
    • The action value q(a): the expected result if the player selects action a and then follows the policy.
    • The advantage \(\pA(a) = \pQ(a) - \pV\): how much better action a is than the average action of the policy.

    An update increases the probability of actions with positive advantage and decreases the others. In chess, repeated updates of this type lead to stronger and stronger play. With hidden information, they often do not.

    4.2Why plain self-play goes in circles

    Think of rock–paper–scissors. If you play rock more often, the best reply is paper. If I play paper more often, your best reply is scissors. Then I move to rock, and the circle starts again. Each player always improves against the other player of the moment. But the pair of policies never settles.

    The bluff game does the same. If you bluff often, standing becomes good for the opponent. If the opponent stands often, bluffing becomes bad for you. You bluff less, so retreating becomes good, so bluffing becomes good again. The paper describes the result as “cyclical, divergent or chaotic learning dynamics”. Figure 4.1 shows them.

    4.3Damping: two knobs that must agree

    Ataraxos damps the dynamics with two knobs. The paper calls the coordination of these knobs its core innovation for reinforcement learning.

    • Regularization strength α. A penalty pulls the policy toward a fixed reference policy, the magnet ρ. For the move network, the magnet selects a movable piece at random and then a legal move at random. The pull keeps the policy varied and unpredictable.
    • Update size η. A second penalty limits how far one update moves the policy from the current policy.

    Both penalties use the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL), a measure of the distance between two probability distributions. The update rule is magnetic mirror descent (MMD), from earlier work by the same first author:

    \[ \pPi_{t+1} = \arg\max_{\pPi}\; \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pPi}\big[\pQ_t(a)\big] \;-\; \pAlpha\,\KL(\pPi,\pRho) \;-\; \tfrac{1}{\pEta}\,\KL(\pPi,\pPi_t) \]

    Get value, stay near the magnet, and move only a little from the current policy.

    \[ \pPi_{t+1}(a) \;\propto\; \Big[\, \pPi_t(a)\; \pRho(a)^{\pAlpha\pEta}\; e^{\pEta\,\pQ_t(a)} \Big]^{\frac{1}{1+\pAlpha\pEta}} \]

    The same update in closed form. Each action’s weight mixes the old policy, the magnet and the action value.

    \[ \pPi^{*}(a) \;\propto\; \pRho(a)\; e^{\pQ^{*}(a)/\pAlpha} \]

    The rest point. Each player’s policy is a “soft” best response. This rest point is a quantal response equilibrium (QRE).

    The quantal response equilibrium comes from economics (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995). In a QRE, players make small mistakes, and costly mistakes are rare. For a two-player zero-sum game, MMD converges to the QRE at an exponential rate. For a small α, the QRE is close to a Nash equilibrium.

    The two knobs must agree. With strong regularization, the dynamics are calm, so large steps are safe. But the rest point is far from the equilibrium, because the magnet pulls too much. With weak regularization, the rest point is close to the equilibrium, but only small steps are safe. Ataraxos therefore uses both schedules together: strong regularization and large updates early, weak regularization and small updates late.

    import math

P_GENERAL, WIN, LOSE, TEMPO = 0.25, 1.0, -0.5, 0.2


def belief(b):
    """Opponent's probability that an advancing piece is the General."""
    return P_GENERAL / (P_GENERAL + (1 - P_GENERAL) * b)


def payoff(b, s):
    """Your expected payoff when you bluff with rate b and the opponent stands with rate s."""
    return P_GENERAL * (s * WIN + (1 - s) * TEMPO) + (1 - P_GENERAL) * b * (s * LOSE + (1 - s) * TEMPO)


def exploitability(b, s):
    """Sum of what each player gains by a switch to a best response."""
    return max(payoff(0, s), payoff(1, s)) - min(payoff(b, 0), payoff(b, 1))


def mmd(p, q1, q0, eta, alpha, magnet=0.5):
    """One MMD step for a two-action policy: p is the probability of action 1."""
    l1 = (math.log(p) + alpha * eta * math.log(magnet) + eta * q1) / (1 + alpha * eta)
    l0 = (math.log(1 - p) + alpha * eta * math.log(1 - magnet) + eta * q0) / (1 + alpha * eta)
    m = max(l1, l0)
    return math.exp(l1 - m) / (math.exp(l1 - m) + math.exp(l0 - m))


def self_play(schedule, steps=400, b=0.85, s=0.85):
    """Both players update at the same time; schedule(t) gives (eta, alpha)."""
    trail = []
    for t in range(steps):
        eta, alpha = schedule(t)
        q_bluff = s * LOSE + (1 - s) * TEMPO           # your Scout: bluff vs stay (0)
        q = belief(b)
        q_stand = -(q * WIN + (1 - q) * LOSE)          # opponent: stand vs retreat
        b, s = mmd(b, q_bluff, 0.0, eta, alpha), mmd(s, q_stand, -TEMPO, eta, alpha)
        b, s = min(max(b, 1e-9), 1 - 1e-9), min(max(s, 1e-9), 1 - 1e-9)
        trail.append((b, s))
    swing = max(x[0] for x in trail[-100:]) - min(x[0] for x in trail[-100:])
    return b, s, exploitability(b, s), swing

    The output repeats the lesson of Figure 4.1 in numbers. Without damping (α = 0), the bluff rate swings over its full range. With strong damping and a matched step, the path stops, but at a biased point. The coordinated schedule ends closest to the equilibrium. If regularization falls faster than the step size, the swings return, and the opponent’s policy collapses to a pure action that is easy to exploit.

    The paper gives its own picture for this. Regularization works “somewhat analogously to an energy reserve”. If you spend it too slowly, the policy stays weak. If you spend it too fast, you get fast early gains, but the policy loses its variety. Then it cannot learn more, and opponents can exploit it.

    A mechanical picture also helps. A car has springs and dampers. Springs without dampers bounce for a long time. Dampers that are too stiff make the ride hard. A good car matches the two. Ataraxos matches α and η in the same way.

    4.4How Ataraxos trains on Stratego

    Stratego has two phases, so Ataraxos has two self-play processes. They learn separately but at the same time. The set-up network makes the starting boards. The move network plays the games from these boards. The results of the games update both networks.

    PartSet-up networkMove network
    ArchitectureDecoder-only transformer. It writes the 40 pieces one at a time, like a language model writes words.Encoder-only transformer. It reads the board and scores all legal moves with a key–query product.

    The data filter is a surprise. Ataraxos ignores the 75% of moves that changed little. This made each iteration approximately 2.5 times faster. It also improved sample efficiency and final strength. The authors say this effect merits further study.

    Deeper

    4.5The move loss, term by term

    The arXiv version of the paper gives the full policy loss of the move network:

    \[ L_{\pPi} = -\min\!\big(r\,\delta,\ \mathrm{clip}(r, 0.8, 1.2)\,\delta\big) \;+\; 0.1\,\KL(\pPi_\theta, \pPi_{\theta_t}) \;+\; \pAlpha\,\KL(\pPi_\theta, \pRho) \]
    • \(r = \pPi_\theta(m)/\pPi_{\theta_t}(m)\) is the ratio of the new to the old probability of the played move m. The clip limits it to [0.8, 1.2]. This is the PPO rule (Schulman and colleagues, 2017).
    • δ is the estimated advantage of the move.
    • The second term limits the update size. It is the step-size knob of MMD.
    • The third term pulls toward the magnet. It is the regularization knob of MMD.

    So the training rule is a deep-learning version of the MMD update in Section 4.3.

    Speed also matters. The team wrote a Stratego simulator in CUDA, the programming system for NVIDIA GPUs. It aims for approximately 10 million board updates per second. It does not store past states. Instead, it can go back in the history of a game and rebuild any past state when the training needs it.

    • Reinforcement learning: 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs for 1 week.
    • Belief network: 4 H100 GPUs for 4 days.
    • Volume: 163 million games and 208 billion simulator steps.
    • Cost: less than US$8,000 at 2025 prices.

    5Playing: beliefs and search

    5.1The belief network

    After training, Ataraxos plays many more self-play games with its final networks. In self-play, the program knows the true ranks of all pieces. So it can train a belief network to predict the hidden ranks from what one player sees.

    The belief network reads the position with a transformer encoder. Then a transformer decoder predicts the ranks of the hidden enemy pieces one at a time, in row order. Each prediction can use the earlier predictions. So the network samples complete, consistent armies, for example with exactly one Marshal. A language model writes a sentence in the same way, one word after the other.

    Two details make it robust. The network trains with “teacher forcing”: during training, each step sees the true earlier ranks. It also uses dropout, which helps it with positions that self-play never produced, such as positions after unusual human moves.

    5.2Search as one more learning step

    Before each move, Ataraxos does four steps:

    1. It samples possible enemy armies from the belief network: approximately 1,000 divided by the number of legal moves.
    2. For each legal move and each sample, it plays a rollout. The rollout makes that move, then plays 39 more moves for both players with the move network. In total, there are 1,000 rollouts of depth 40.
    3. It averages the value predictions at the ends of the rollouts. This gives an estimate \(\hat{\pQ}(m)\) for each move m.
    4. It makes one MMD update for this decision only, and samples its move from the result.
    \[ \pPi_{\text{search}}(m) \;\propto\; \Big[\, e^{\hat{\pQ}(m)}\; \pRho(m)^{\pAlpha}\; \pPi_\theta(m)^{\pBeta} \Big]^{\frac{1}{\pAlpha+\pBeta}}, \qquad \pAlpha = 0.002,\ \pBeta = 0.02 \]

    Here β is the strength of a leash to the move network’s own policy \(\pPi_\theta\). The search takes approximately 1.26 seconds per move on one H100. This is faster than human play.

    The idea comes from the update-equivalence framework (Sokota and colleagues, ICLR 2024). Earlier search methods for imperfect information built a “subgame” over all hidden states. That subgame grows with the hidden information. Update equivalence asks a different question: what would one more step of self-play learning change at this decision? Because the search copies a learning step, it keeps the improvement properties of learning.

    Three facts make the step safe:

    • The rollouts use the move network for both players, and each player sees only their own information. So no player cheats inside a rollout, and strategy fusion does not occur.
    • The belief network models the self-play policy, and the rollouts use the self-play policy. So the averages estimate self-play action values, whatever the real opponent does.
    • The update changes only the current decision. It cannot damage the policy at other positions. So it can be larger than a training update.

    5.3How much the search adds

    The arXiv version of the paper reports the Elo rating of the search under different settings. Elo is a rating scale from chess. A difference of 100 points means the stronger player scores approximately 64% of the points.

    The last bar is the most important lesson of search. Without the leash β, the search trusts its noisy averages too much. It then exploits mistakes of its own move network in the rollouts, and these mistakes do not exist in real opponents. The authors also note a limit: one update step can only improve the policy by a bounded amount. A search that improves without limit with more compute is future work.

    6Results

    6.1The match against Pim Niemeijer

    Pim Niemeijer has 4 world titles, 15 Dutch titles, 2 online world titles, and more than 600 weeks as number 1. George Franka, the only player who has competed in every world championship since 1997, calls him “the best Stratego player ever”.

    The match had 20 games over 3 weeks on the website Strategus, with 15 minutes plus 3 seconds per move for each player. Pim knew that Ataraxos would not adapt to his play. So he could look for a weakness and use it again and again. Three-time world champion Vincent de Boer called this a large handicap for the AI. Pim received US$1,000, plus US$100 for each win and US$50 for each draw.

    An 85% effective win rate is without precedent at the top level. Max Roelofs, three times the world runner-up, says that margins between top players are “razor thin”, because each game requires risk.

    6.2How Ataraxos plays

    Top players noted these differences from human play:

    • Set-ups. More strong pieces at the front, more Bombs in the third and fourth rows, and more Flags in back corners. Its set-ups have less predictable structure. It uses a Flag with Bombs around it in approximately two thirds of games.
    • Hard to read. Its moves give less information about the ranks of its pieces.
    • Calm about draws. It accepts an early draw if more play has negative expected value. This happened in game 11 against Pim.
    • Patient and stubborn. It keeps Scouts late in the game. When it is behind, it stalls and pesters to slow the opponent. Some people called this rude.
    • Bluffs. It uses some common bluffs rarely, and other bluffs that humans think are too risky.
    • Strong in the long run. Players praised its positional play, its defence, its play with less information, and its change from middle game to end game.
    • “Preternaturally lucky”. It always seemed to have the right piece in the right place. Good beliefs and a low-exploitability policy can look like luck.

    6.3The same recipe in three other games

    The team applied the same techniques to three more games. Each game tests a different structure.

    • Barrage Stratego (zero-sum). Each player has only 8 pieces, so each game has more risk and more bluffs. Ataraxos won all four 50-game series against three of the four top-ranked players. Each of these players is a two-time Barrage world champion. The first three series used the policy network without search, because the belief network was still in training. The search then beat world number 1 Pim Niemeijer 31–14 (5 draws).
    • Hanabi (cooperative). Players see the cards of their teammates but not their own. They give each other limited hints. Ataraxos set a new state of the art for 2 to 5 players. For 2 players, it used approximately 100 times less compute than the earlier best method.
    • Dou dizhu (team). One “landlord” plays against two “peasants” who cooperate without talk. Ataraxos beat the earlier best programs, PerfectDou and DouZero.

    6.4Cost, compared with DeepNash

    Ataraxos used approximately 1/500 of the compute cost, 1/30 of the self-play games, and 1/100 of the training examples of DeepNash. The smaller numbers of games and examples show that the gain is not only fast code. The method learns more from each game.

    The two systems never played each other. The authors asked DeepMind for a match. DeepMind replied that the DeepNash code no longer works.

    The DeepNash evaluation in 2022 was also different. Its human opponents on the Gravon website did not know that they played a bot. Also, most top players no longer used that site. At the 2023 World Championship, DeepNash lost to most of the top players, Pim among them.

    7Misconceptions

    Looks likeImperfect information means chance.
    ActuallyThey are different properties.
    BecauseBackgammon has chance but perfect information. After the set-up, Stratego has no chance, but it has imperfect information.
    Looks likeComplete information and perfect information are the same.
    ActuallyStratego has complete but imperfect information.
    Because“Complete” is about knowledge of the rules and payoffs. “Perfect” is about the visibility of earlier events.
    Looks likeMake the best guess of the hidden pieces, then play as in chess.
    ActuallyThis causes strategy fusion.
    BecauseA plan for a known world can use information that a real player never gets (Figure 5.1, part A).
    Looks likeRandom choices are a weakness.
    ActuallyA good policy for hidden information must be mixed.
    BecauseAn opponent can exploit any fixed pattern (Figure 3.1).
    Looks likeSelf-play always makes a program stronger.
    ActuallyPlain self-play can circle or diverge.
    BecauseEach player chases the other player’s latest policy (Figure 4.1).
    Looks likeRegularization only costs strength.
    ActuallyIt is the damper that makes learning converge.
    BecauseIt must fall over the training run, at a rate that matches the step size.
    Looks likeMore trust in the search is always better.
    ActuallyWithout a leash, search falls from 2218 to 1733 Elo.
    BecauseNoisy averages overfit to the quirks of the network’s own rollouts (Figure 5.2).
    Looks likeAtaraxos reads its opponent and adapts.
    ActuallyIt plays a fixed policy.
    BecauseIts goal is low exploitability, not exploitation of one person.

    8Limits and open questions

    • Search has a ceiling. One update step can give only a bounded improvement. More compute at play time cannot continue to help. The authors suggest ideas from knowledge-limited subgame solving, the method behind the 2025 Fog-of-War chess program Obscuro.
    • Memory of the game. The networks see history only through hand-made features, such as “this piece moved”. Attention over time helped the belief network, but it was too slow for reinforcement learning at this budget.
    • A fast simulator is necessary. The method needs millions of games. The paper says the approach fits problems “for which fast, accurate simulators can be constructed”. Many real negotiations and markets do not have one.
    • Small-sample evaluation. Twenty games against one person are strong evidence, but they are not independent samples.
    • Two-player zero-sum theory. The guarantees of MMD are for two-player zero-sum games. Hanabi and dou dizhu worked well in practice, but the theory there is weaker.
    • Interpretability. MIT News reports that the team wants to make the AI’s reasons visible before people use it in fields such as negotiation, cybersecurity or finance.

    10Connections and analogies

    • Poker. Poker AI showed that bluffs can come from mathematics, not from psychology. Ataraxos keeps the goal (low exploitability) but drops the public-belief-state machinery that does not scale.
    • Bridge and Battleship. Bridge programs met strategy fusion first. Battleship is the simplest picture of hidden set-ups: the arrangement is one large secret decision, as in Stratego.
    • Fog-of-War chess and Kriegspiel. These are chess variants with hidden enemy moves. Obscuro (2025) reached superhuman level with a different search method. The Ataraxos authors see its ideas as a route to stronger search.
    • Diplomacy. CICERO (Meta, 2022) also used a KL leash, toward a model of human play. The leash appears again and again in modern AI.
    • Language models. RLHF and PPO keep a KL penalty to a reference model, as Ataraxos does. The belief network decodes armies piece by piece, as a language model decodes words. The paper also notes that Monte Carlo advantages worked well in DeepSeek-R1, as they did for the Ataraxos set-up network.
    • Control engineering. Damping, overshoot and stability are old ideas in control. Figure 4.1 is a small stability diagram for learning.
    • Economics. The quantal response equilibrium came from studies of real human errors in games. Ataraxos uses it as a stepping stone toward Nash.

    11Synthesis

    With hidden information, the value of a decision depends on decisions before it, after it, and beside it. Older methods handle this with lists over all hidden states, and these lists do not scale. Ataraxos keeps the simple recipe of chess and Go, with three changes. It damps self-play with matched regularization and step size, so that the current policy converges. It learns a belief network, so that it can sample the hidden information. And it makes search into one more damped learning step, with a leash to its own network.

    Key relations

    \[ \pPi_{t+1} \propto \big[\pPi_t\,\pRho^{\pAlpha\pEta}\,e^{\pEta\pQ_t}\big]^{\frac{1}{1+\pAlpha\pEta}} \qquad \pBel(\pH\mid\pO) \propto \pBel(\pO\mid\pH)\,\pBel(\pH) \qquad \pPi_{\text{search}} \propto \big[e^{\hat{\pQ}}\,\pRho^{\pAlpha}\,\pPi_\theta^{\pBeta}\big]^{\frac{1}{\pAlpha+\pBeta}} \]

    The picture to keep is a path in the bluff square. Without damping, the path spirals out to the corners. When the two knobs fall together, the path settles near the equilibrium.

    Transfer question

    A company wants to use this recipe for a sealed-bid auction against other companies. Which parts transfer directly? What does the company need before it can start?

    Show an answer

    The ideas transfer: a mixed policy, a belief over the hidden values of the other bidders, damped self-play, and search as one update. But the company first needs a fast and accurate simulator of the auction and of the other bidders. The paper names this condition. An auction with more than two bidders is also not two-player zero-sum, so the convergence guarantee of MMD does not apply directly.

    11.1Exercises

    1. In the bluff game, the prior chance of a General changes from 0.25 to 0.4. Does the equilibrium bluff rate go up or down?
      Answer

      Up. The opponent stays indifferent only if the belief after an advance is 0.467. With more Generals, you can add more bluffs before the belief falls to that level: 0.4 / (0.4 + 0.6 b) = 0.467 gives b ≈ 0.76.

    2. Barrage series: the AI won 122, lost 64 and drew 14. With the same caveats as above, is a one-sided sign test significant at 0.001?
      Answer

      Yes. The probability of 122 or more wins in 186 decisive games with a fair coin is approximately 1.3 × 10-5, as the paper reports.

    3. Why does the search use a smaller regularization toward the magnet (α = 0.002) than the training rule?
      Answer

      The search update affects only one decision and uses more accurate estimates. It does not need the same protection against cycles across the whole policy. The leash β to the network policy controls the remaining risk.
