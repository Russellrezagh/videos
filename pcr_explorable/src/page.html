The Polymerase Chain Reaction

How a tube that only ever gets hot, cooler and warm again turns one chosen stretch of DNA into a billion copies, how to count them, and how to make the counting tell you how much DNA you started with.

Prose, equations, figures, a narrated animation and five experiments, all drawn from one model of the reaction.

§ 1 · Orientationprose

One sentence, then four ideas you need

PCR copies one chosen stretch of DNA again and again, and every copy made so far becomes a template for the next round. Because the number of templates doubles each round, thirty rounds turn one molecule into about a billion.

That is why PCR sits underneath so much of biology and medicine: testing a swab for a virus, matching a trace of blood at a crime scene, checking an embryo for a disease variant, cloning a gene, or preparing DNA for sequencing. Each needs more copies of one stretch of DNA than nature provides.

You need four ideas first. Each one returns many times below.

  1. DNA is two strands that run in opposite directions. Each strand has a 5′ end and a 3′ end, and the two are paired base by base: A with T, G with C.
  2. Heat separates the strands; cooling lets matching sequences pair again. The temperature at which half of a pair has come apart is its melting temperature, Tm.
  3. A polymerase can only extend a strand that already exists. It needs a short paired stretch with a free 3′ end to start from, which is what a primer is.
  4. Repeated doubling is exponential. Ten doublings make about a thousand, twenty about a million, thirty about a billion.

Mental model. Separate, bind, copy, repeat. The primers decide what is copied. The cycle count decides how many times. The efficiency decides how fast the copies pile up.

    § 3 · Ingredientslabelled figure

    Six things in a tube, one machine around it

    A PCR tube holds a few tens of microlitres. In it are the template DNA, two primers, a heat-stable DNA polymerase, the four dNTPs, Mg²⁺ ions and a buffer. Around it, a thermal cycler changes the temperature on a fixed program. Nothing else is added once the run starts.

    Two of these deserve a second look. The primers are the only part you design: their sequences decide which stretch of the template is copied, and nothing outside the two primer sites ever is. The polymerase must survive near-boiling temperatures thirty times over. The enzyme used most often, Taq, comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium isolated from a hot spring in Yellowstone in 1969 fact.

    Before Taq. The first PCR experiments used a polymerase from E. coli, which heat destroys. Fresh enzyme had to be added by hand after every heating step. A heat-stable enzyme made PCR something a machine could do alone.

    § 4 · Mechanismnarrated animation

    One cycle, and then three

    A cycle has three steps, each at its own temperature: denature (about 95 °C), anneal (about 55–65 °C) and extend (72 °C). Watch one template go through them. The animation is narrated; press play, or step through it at your own pace.

    After cycle 1 there are two double strands, but the new strands are too long. In which cycle does the first double strand with exactly the target length appear?

    Cycle 3. In cycle 2 the first strands with the exact length are made, but each is still paired with a longer template strand. Only in cycle 3 does an exact strand get an exact partner.

    Notice what never happens: nothing outside the two primer sites is ever copied on purpose, and the original template is never consumed. It stays in the tube, making one more long copy every cycle.

    § 5 · Counting the strandsderivation + interactive

    Why the exact copies win

    Give the three kinds of strand names. O is an original strand, longer than the target on both sides. L is a long new strand: it starts at a primer but runs past the far primer site. S is a short new strand, exactly primer to primer. With perfect copying, every strand serves as a template once per cycle, and the kind of copy depends only on the kind of template:

    \text{O} \;\to\; \text{L}, \qquad \text{L} \;\to\; \text{S}, \qquad \text{S} \;\to\; \text{S}

    That is all the biology. The rest is counting. Starting from one double-stranded template, write On, Ln and Sn for the number of strands of each kind after cycle n. Step through the derivation:

    1. O_n = 2The two original strands are never used up.
    2. L_n = L_{n-1} + O_{n-1} = 2nEach cycle, each original makes one more long strand: two per cycle.
    3. S_n = 2\,S_{n-1} + L_{n-1}Every short strand is copied (doubling them), and every long strand yields one short copy.
    4. S_n = 2^{n+1} - 2n - 2Check it: 2(2ⁿ − 2(n−1) − 2) + 2(n−1) = 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 2n − 2.
    5. \text{amplicons after cycle } n \;=\; S_{n-1} \;=\; 2^{n} - 2nAn exact double strand is a short template paired with its short copy.

    Stuck?

    The long strands grow by two per cycle, a straight line. The amplicons double. Linear growth cannot keep up with doubling, so after a handful of cycles almost every molecule in the tube is an exact copy. Drive it yourself:

    § 6 · The thermal programinteractive
    
  
    Choosing the three temperatures
    
  
    Denaturation at 94–98 °C separates the strands; the first one of a run is held longer (2–5 minutes) so that even long genomic DNA comes fully apart. Extension at 72 °C is close to the temperature where Taq works fastest; protocols allow about one minute per thousand bases of target rule of thumb. The step that needs judgement is annealing.
    
  
    At the annealing temperature Ta the primers must find their sites. Too hot, and they do not stay bound long enough to be extended: the yield collapses. Too cold, and they also stick to sites that match only partly: you copy the wrong things, and primers stick to each other (primer-dimers). The usual starting point is a few degrees below the primers’ melting temperature rule of thumb.
    
  
    
  
    A teaching model. The binding curves treat each primer site as a two-state switch with a sigmoid melting curve, and model off-target sites as many weaker matches with a lower Tm. Real tubes add salt, magnesium and sequence effects; the shape of the trade-off is the point, not the exact numbers.
    

    


    
  
    § 7 · Exponential growthequations + worked example
    
  
    Efficiency, and why growth stops
    
  
    Real reactions do not double perfectly. Call the fraction of templates copied in one cycle the efficiency E: one cycle multiplies the number of copies by 1 + E. After n cycles, starting from N0 copies:
    
  
    N_n \;=\; N_0\,(1+\htmlData{obj=efficiency}{E})^{\htmlData{obj=cycle}{n}}
    
  
    Taking logarithms turns the question “how many cycles?” into a division:
    
  
    n \;=\; \frac{\log\left(N/N_0\right)}{\log(1+E)}
    
  
    Worked example
    
  
    A swab holds about 100 copies of a viral target, and the assay runs at E = 0.9. How many cycles until there are 1010 copies, enough to see?
    
  
      
    
    1. \tfrac{N}{N_0} = \tfrac{10^{10}}{10^{2}} = 10^{8}Only the ratio matters.
      2. 
    
    2. \log_{10}(10^8) = 8, \quad \log_{10}(1.9) \approx 0.2788Any base works, as long as both logs use it.
      3. 
    
    3. n = 8 / 0.2788 \approx 28.7 \text{ cycles}At perfect doubling it would be 8 / 0.301 ≈ 26.6: ten percent less efficiency costs two cycles.
      4. 
  
    
  
    
    
    Two samples run side by side. Sample B started with ten times less DNA. At E = 1, how much later does it reach the same amount?
    
    
    
    
    About 3.3 cycles: it needs log(10) / log(2) ≈ 3.32 extra doublings to make up a factor of ten. This number is the heart of real-time PCR in § 8.
    
  
    
  
    Why growth stops. Exponential growth cannot last. After 25–35 cycles the reaction reaches a plateau: primers and dNTPs run low, the polymerase becomes the bottleneck, and the abundant product strands re-pair with each other instead of with primers. Once on the plateau, tubes that started with very different amounts end with similar amounts fact. That is why the brightness of a band at the end of a run says little about how much DNA you started with.
    
  
    The plateau model used on this page is a discrete logistic: each cycle multiplies by 1 + E(1 − N/K), where K is the most the tube can hold. It reproduces the shape, not the chemistry.
    

    


    
  
    § 8 · Real-time PCRinteractive + worked example
    
  
    Turning cycles into amounts
    
  
    Real-time (quantitative) PCR measures the product after every cycle. A dye such as SYBR Green glows when it binds double-stranded DNA, or a sequence-specific probe (TaqMan) releases a fluorescent reporter when the polymerase degrades it. Either way, the fluorescence follows the number of copies.
    
  
    Set a threshold above the background noise. The fractional cycle at which a sample crosses it is its threshold cycle, Ct. Because every sample is measured at the same amount, the threshold NT, the growth law gives:
    
  
    \htmlData{obj=ct}{C_t} \;=\; \frac{\log N_T - \log N_0}{\log(1+E)}
    
  
    Solve for the starting amount:
    
  
    \log_{10} N_0 \;=\; \log_{10} N_T \;-\; \htmlData{obj=ct}{C_t}\,\log_{10}(1+E)
    
  
    Read it left to right: Ct is a straight-line function of the logarithm of the starting amount, with a negative slope. More template, earlier Ct. Ten times more template, about 3.32 cycles earlier at perfect efficiency. Plot Ct against log10N0 for samples of known concentration and you get a standard curve; its slope m gives the efficiency:
    
  
    m = -\frac{1}{\log_{10}(1+E)} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad E = 10^{-1/m} - 1
    
  
    
  
    Worked example: an unknown sample
    
  
    Four standards, 106 down to 103 copies, give Ct values of 15.1, 18.4, 21.8 and 25.1. The unknown crosses at Ct = 20.0.
    
  
      
  
      Most assays are accepted when the slope lies between about −3.6 and −3.1 (E from 90 to 110 %) and the points fit a line closely (R² above about 0.98) common acceptance range. An apparent efficiency above 100 % usually signals a problem, such as inhibitors in the concentrated standards or primer-dimers adding signal.
      
  
      Relative quantification: ΔΔCt
    
      Often you do not need copy numbers, only “how much more is this gene expressed after treatment?” Compare the target with a reference gene in both conditions. With both assays near E = 1, the fold change is
      
    
      \text{fold change} = 2^{-\Delta\Delta C_t}, \qquad \Delta\Delta C_t = (C_t^{\text{target}} - C_t^{\text{ref}})_{\text{treated}} - (C_t^{\text{target}} - C_t^{\text{ref}})_{\text{control}}
      
    
      If the efficiencies are not close to 1, replace 2 with 1 + E; the method assumes both assays have the same efficiency.
      
  
      

      


      
  
      § 9 · Primer designinteractive + code
      
  
      Two short strands decide everything
      
  
      A primer pair defines the product: the forward primer has the sequence of the top strand at the left end of the target, and the reverse primer has the reverse complement of the top strand at the right end. Good pairs follow a short list of rules of thumb rules of thumb: 18–25 bases, 40–60 % G + C, a melting temperature around 52–65 °C, the two Tm within about 5 °C of each other, one or two G or C bases at the 3′ end, and no complementarity between 3′ ends.
      
  
      The 3′ end matters most, because the polymerase extends from it. A mismatch there blocks extension; a mismatch, or even a whole extra tail, at the 5′ end usually does not. Labs use that freedom to add restriction sites and adapters to the ends of products.
      
  
      
  
      The page estimates Tm three ways. The Wallace rule (2 °C per A·T, 4 °C per G·C) is quick and rough. The GC formula adds length. The nearest-neighbour method sums the stacking energies of each neighbouring pair of bases (SantaLucia’s unified parameters) and is the one modern design tools use fact.       § 10 · Reading the resultstatic figure
      
  
      Did it work? The gel
      
  
      For end-point PCR the usual check is agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA is negatively charged, so in an electric field it moves through the gel toward the positive electrode, and shorter fragments move faster. A ladder of fragments of known sizes runs beside the samples. A clean PCR shows one sharp band at the expected length, and nothing in the no-template control.
      
  
  
      Real-time PCR with a DNA dye adds a second check after the run: heat the products slowly and watch the fluorescence drop as they melt. A single product melts at one temperature and gives one peak; primer-dimers melt lower and show up as a second, earlier peak.

      Digital PCR: counting with Poisson
      
  
      Split a sample into many partitions and run PCR in all of them. A partition lights up if it received at least one copy. Copies land at random, so the number per partition follows a Poisson distribution with mean λ, and the fraction of empty partitions is e−λ. Count the positive fraction p and invert:
      
  
      p = 1 - e^{-\lambda} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda = -\ln(1 - p)

      § 13 · Edge cases and failure modesprose + numbers
      
  
      Where exponential copying bites back
      
  
      Contamination. The property that makes PCR sensitive makes it fragile. One stray molecule from an earlier product, copied at E = 0.95 for 35 cycles, becomes 1.9535 ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ copies. Labs separate set-up from analysis physically, use filter tips, and run a no-template control in every batch.
      
  
      Very little template. At a few copies per tube, chance decides what goes into each tube. If a sample holds on average λ copies per reaction volume, a reaction receives none with probability e−λ: with λ = 3, about 5 % of reactions get none and come out negative though the sample is positive. Replicates and digital PCR deal with this.
      
  
      Inhibitors. Substances carried over from the sample (heme from blood, humic acids from soil, heparin, leftover ethanol) slow the polymerase. Efficiency drops, Ct shifts later, and quantities come out too low. A diluted sample that gives an earlier Ct than expected is a sign of inhibition.
      
  
      Difficult templates. GC-rich stretches hold their strands together and fold into hairpins; additives such as DMSO or betaine help. Long targets (beyond a few kilobases) need longer extension and enzyme blends.
      
  
      Copying errors. Taq has no proofreading, so it occasionally inserts a wrong base, and an early error is copied into every descendant. For cloning or sequencing, use a proofreading polymerase.
      
  
      The plateau hides differences. End-point brightness is not a measure of starting amount (§ 7). Quantify in the exponential phase, which is what Ct does.
      

      


      
  
      § 14 · History and connectionstimeline
      
  
      From a hot spring to every hospital
      
  
  
      Connections. Cells copy DNA with the same chemistry: a polymerase extending a primer from its 3′ end. The cell separates strands with an enzyme (helicase) where PCR uses heat, and it makes short RNA primers where PCR adds DNA ones. Sanger sequencing is a single primer extension with chain-terminating nucleotides. Sequencing libraries are amplified by PCR. Isothermal methods such as LAMP amplify at one temperature and power many rapid tests, and CRISPR-based diagnostics often amplify first and detect with a Cas enzyme after.
      

      


      
  
      § 15 · Synthesissummary card
      
  
      What you now know
      
  
      
    
      The idea. PCR repeats a three-temperature cycle. Primers choose the stretch, the polymerase copies from each primer, and every copy becomes a template. Exact copies appear in cycle 3 and then double, while the long by-products only grow by two per cycle.
      
    
      The equations. Nn = N0(1 + E)n while growth is exponential. In real-time PCR, Ct falls on a straight line against log N0 with slope −1/log10(1 + E): about −3.32 per factor of ten when E = 1.
      
    
      The picture to keep. A doubling population of exact copies overtaking a straight line of long ones, and a family of S-shaped curves, evenly spaced, one per tenfold dilution.
      
  
      
  
      
    
      Transfer: a positive control with 10⁴ copies crosses the threshold at Ct 25. A patient sample crosses at Ct 30, in the same run, E = 0.95. Roughly how many copies did the sample have?
      
    
      
    
      Five cycles later means 1.95⁵ ≈ 28 times less template: 10⁴ / 28 ≈ 355 copies.
